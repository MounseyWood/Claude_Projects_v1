By the end, you can

  1. Explain how PET becomes fibre: polymerise, melt-spin, draw.
  2. Link fibre form to performance: cross-section, filament count, filament versus staple, twist.
  3. Explain how texturing and plying change a yarn: loops and crimp for bulk, balanced twist for a calm, strong thread.
  4. Compare two ways of colouring polyester and what each costs.
  5. Explain why blends with natural fibres or elastane block recycling, and judge claims about alternatives.
  6. Compare mechanical and chemical recycling: what each keeps, what each costs, and what is proven at scale.
  7. Explain why cotton rots while polyester breaks up into microplastics, and question "biodegradable" claims.

How this lab works

  • Give it about 20 minutes, ideally on a phone. Nothing needs an account.
  • Do something to find something out. Facts arrive as discovery cards when you act. A wrong move gets a question back, not the answer.
  • Predict first. Guess before you check: the 59% and 1.35% squares, the nozzle tests, the recycler, the fork, the heat, the burial.
  • Your thread keeps every choice. Shape, filaments, texture, twist, plies, wrap, colour, washing and recycling all stay with it. The care label at the end reads it back.
  • Collect the set. The counter on the progress bar opens your discoveries. Gaps show which page still hides one.
  • Stuck? If a page sits still, a pixel hand shows you the gesture.
  • Before you leave, check yourself on the last pages, then use Reflect and copy. Nothing is saved when you close the page.

Why it looks like this

Every shade is woven. The dithered tones are point-paper weave structures: spot, twill and plain weave. It nods to Jacquard's punched cards (1804), which taught looms, and later computers, to follow a pattern [36]. Students who tap the spool on the first page find this as a hidden discovery.

Each chapter moves the thread its own way, as the Line in The Dot and the Line shows character through shape: Make steps like pipework, Fibre curves like optics, Yarn coils, loops and stitches, Combine drapes and waves, and Return breaks into zigzags and sags. The chain rises like a molecule; the cut is a slash across the screen.

Questions to think about

For your reflection, or to go further.

  1. Your leggings say "78% recycled polyester, 22% elastane". Where can they go at the end of their life?
  2. You need a warm, light jacket fill and a cool running top. Which nozzle for each, and why?
  3. Microfibre feels softest. Is that worth more shedding? For which garment?
  4. Solution dyeing claims big water savings. What evidence would convince you, and who should provide it?
  5. Redesign one garment you own as a mono-material. What do you lose? What do you gain?
  6. Synthetic fibre uses about 1.35% of the world's oil. Small or large? What else would you need to know: tonnes, growth, the energy used to make it?
  7. Chemical recycling can separate blends. Does that make blends acceptable again? What would you need to know first?
  8. A label says "biodegradable polyester". What three questions would you ask, and who should answer them?
  9. Cotton rots in a compost heap, but most clothes end up in landfill or are burnt. Does "it rots" still help there? What would you need to find out?

How sure are we?

  • Strong 59% of fibre is polyester; 88% fossil-based; under 1% of fibre from recycled textiles [1].
  • Strong 78 million tonnes of polyester fibre in 2024; 12% recycled [1].
  • Moderate Synthetic fibres use about 1.35% of global oil consumption: one NGO estimate (Changing Markets, 2021) [33]. A refinery sends roughly 10–20% of a barrel to all petrochemicals [35]. These measure different things, so never multiply them.
  • Strong Terephthalic acid comes from oil (paraxylene); ethylene glycol comes from ethylene, made from oil naphtha or natural-gas ethane [34].
  • Strong Invented 1941 by Whinfield and Dickson [2][3].
  • Strong Jacquard's punched-card loom, 1804–05; Babbage adopted punched cards about 1830; Lovelace's "weaves algebraical patterns" [36].
  • Strong Trilobal sparkles, hollow insulates, multichannel wicks [13][14].
  • Strong Microfibre: under 1 decitex per filament [15].
  • Strong Linking terephthalic acid and ethylene glycol gives off water [27].
  • Strong Air-jet texturing blows multifilament yarn into loops for bulk [25]; false-twist texturing gives crimp [26].
  • Moderate "Much clothing polyester is false-twist textured": a 1985 patent puts it above 70%; no recent figure found [26].
  • Strong Plying opposite to the singles twist balances a yarn; sewing threads are 2, 3 or 4 ply [24].
  • Strong Single yarns are usually spun Z and plied S [24].
  • Strong Polyester is thermoplastic, so heat-set pleats are permanent; Pleats Please garments are sewn, then pleated with heat [37]. Heat-setting runs at about 170 to 210°C (Moderate, trade source) [38].
  • Strong PLA composts only in industrial conditions (about 58°C and above), not at home [39]. Ordinary PET barely biodegrades: in one maker's accelerated sea-water test, just over 1% in about 2.7 years (Weak) [40].
  • Strong Cotton biodegrades and polyester barely does. In a compost test, cotton fabrics lost 50–77% of their weight in 90 days while polyester kept its structure [41]. In water tests, cotton yarn was 76% broken down after 243 days and polyester under 5%; in a polyester/cotton blend, mainly the cotton broke down [42][43]. These studies were co-authored by Cotton Incorporated, a cotton industry body, and peer reviewed.
  • Moderate Simulated sunlight cracked PET fibres into smaller particles within 56 days, in seawater, in the lab [44]. PET bottles on the sea floor stayed largely unchanged for about 15 years, with ages estimated from printed dates (one study) [45].
  • Strong A bacterium from a Japanese PET recycling site digested thin, low-crystallinity PET film in about six weeks at 30°C [46]. Bottle and textile PET is far more crystalline, and most known enzymes struggle with it [49].
  • Strong UK advertising guidance: "biodegradable" and "compostable" claims should say how long, in what conditions, and any harmful by-products [47]. The CMA's Green Claims Code: claims must not omit or hide important information [48].
  • Moderate "No one has yet seen a polyester thread fully decompose" is reasoning, not a measurement: polyester dates from 1941 [2][3], and tests run for months or years, not lifetimes.
  • Moderate Staple often cut near cotton length, around 32–38 mm [16].
  • Moderate Core-spun thread: polyester filament core, cotton or polyester wrap [17].
  • Strong Polyester dyes with disperse dyes at around 130°C under pressure [18].
  • Strong Polyester/cotton blends usually need two dye classes, disperse and reactive, often in two baths [23].
  • Weak Solution dyeing water savings: figures come from producers [19].
  • Moderate 6,000,000+ microfibres from one 5 kg wash; fleece shed most; high-twist yarns shed less [5][20].
  • Moderate Elastane in ~80% of discarded US clothes: one estimate [6].
  • Strong In stretch yarn, elastane usually forms the core, with cotton or filament spun or wound round it (core-spun or covered yarn) [50].
  • Moderate No scalable fibre-to-fibre route for polycotton yet; one plant due 2028 [21].
  • Moderate Dyes compromise recycled polyester; removal still in research [22].
  • Moderate Repeated melting shortens PET chains and lowers its properties [28].
  • Strong Engineered enzyme: 90% of PET broken down in 10 hours, lab scale, 2020 [29].
  • Moderate First full-size enzymatic plant planned for 2028; its financing was not closed by September 2026 [30].
  • Moderate Glycolysis of mixed textiles: cotton survives, polyester and elastane break down, dyes slow it (one lab study) [31].
  • Weak Chemical recycling's footprint: depends on plant and energy; studies disagree [32].
  • Moderate 0.1% PLA spoils PET: bottle recycling, 2009 [7].
  • Weak Two-polyester stretch fibre recycles with polyester: the maker's own tests [8][9].
  • Strong PLA melts ~170°C, PET ~250°C [10]. All-polyester rubbers reach ~11× length in the lab [11].
  • Strong EU textile producer responsibility in force 17 October 2025 [12].

How the thread works

Each exhibit's thread is a chain of points. Every step, each point keeps moving the way it was already moving, gravity and a breeze nudge it, and the links are pulled back to their proper length ten times over: Verlet integration. To draw a shape, each point is pulled a little towards a target drawing. Text blocks are walls.
