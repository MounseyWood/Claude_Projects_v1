FN4005 · Animated explainer · Storyboard and script

The Long Chain

A short animation for first-year fashion students. What polyester is, what it does well and badly, why stretchy clothes rarely get recycled, and what researchers are trying instead. Styled after Saul Bass title sequences: flat colour, cut paper, and one red thread that keeps changing shape.

Runtime 4:32 Shots 17 Parts 5 Voice-over ≈ 560 words Draft v1 · October 2026
Matthew Mounsey-Wood FHEA MA (RCA) LCF Alumni
Before the storyboard

Brief

One thing to remember

A garment can only come back if we know what it's made of, and how it gets back.

Audience

FN4005 undergraduates, Level 4. No chemistry assumed. Most will own polyester and elastane without knowing it, so the care label is the way in.

By the end, students can

  1. Say what polyester is: PET, made mostly from oil, melt-spun into fibre.
  2. Weigh its pros and cons in apparel.
  3. Explain why blends, and elastane in particular, block recycling, and what "mono-material" means.
  4. Describe two frontier routes to stretch, and ask what evidence backs each claim.

Tone

Curious and even-handed. Polyester is neither villain nor saviour. The film models critical reading: it says who made each claim, and stops where the evidence stops.

Style guide

Visual language

  1. One thread is the main character. A single red line appears in every shot: it becomes a molecule, a filament, a recycling loop and, at the end, a loop that doesn't quite close.
  2. Flat colour, no gradients, no 3D. Five film colours plus paper. Each colour always means the same material (see key).
  3. Cut-paper edges. Shapes slightly irregular, type set letter by letter with small rotations, as if cut with scissors.
  4. Type is part of the picture. Words enter on the beat and sit inside the composition, not as subtitles.
  5. Asymmetry and empty space. One idea per frame. Big shapes, lots of ground.
  6. Cut on the beat. Jazz percussion and double bass drive the edit. Homage to the method, not a copy of any Bass sequence.
Colour and shape key · constant in every shot
Red squares · PET polyester
Light red · a second polyester (PTT), only in shot 13
Mustard bars · elastane (polyurethane)
Teal circles · PLA and other plant-based polyesters
Black · oil, fossil feedstock, the machine
Grey · other fibres, waste

Shape carries the meaning as well as colour, so red and teal never rely on hue alone. That keeps the film readable for colour-blind viewers.

What changed from the first draft

Corrections and context

Corrections

  • "Same polyester family" does not mean "same recycling stream". PLA is a known contaminant of PET recycling [8]. The draft treated this as a footnote; the film makes it a main point (shot 12).
  • Sorona® evidence was a supplier's sales page. Replaced with the bicomponent mechanism from the fibre maker, and its recycling claim labelled as the maker's.
  • EcoLASTANE is historical. It was an EU FP7 project (a programme that closed to new projects in 2013). Its title refers to furanic monomers, which suggests PEF-type chemistry, not a drop-in copy of PET. The CORDIS record couldn't be opened from here; worth checking before citing.
  • The PLA elastic-yarn patent dates from 2011. A patent shows an idea was claimed, not that a product exists.
  • Left out as unverified: market-report claims about a Toray PLA/PBAT stretch fibre, a Danimer cross-linked PLA fibre, and PLA price premiums. They came from a market-research site with no primary source.

Context worth knowing (not in the film)

  • Textile-to-textile polyester recycling is still scaling, slowly. Syre reports multi-tonne pilot output in 2025, and plans to start building a plant of up to 250,000 t/yr in Vietnam in 2027 [16]. Carbios has missed its financing target for its Longlaville enzymatic plant [17].
  • PEF, another plant-based polyester. Avantium's FDCA plant produced first batches, with commercial sales expected from late 2026 [18]. Mainly aimed at packaging so far; a possible future fibre topic.
  • Mono-material helps most within one group. Cellulose, polyamide and polyester mono-streams have the most recycling options [19].
After the screening

Seminar questions

Discuss

  1. Your leggings say "78% recycled polyester, 22% elastane". Where can they go at end of life? What would you need to find out?
  2. Polyester needs less water than cotton but comes from oil. How would you decide which is better for one particular garment?
  3. PLA is compostable, but only industrially. Is that a real benefit for a UK wardrobe? Why or why not?
  4. A brand says its stretch fibre is "recyclable". What evidence would you ask for, and from whom?
  5. A report says recycled polyester sheds more microplastics than virgin [6]. Who funded it, how was it tested, and how much weight should it carry?

Make

  1. Pick one stretch garment you own. Redesign it as a mono-material. List what you lose and what you gain.
  2. Draw a care label that also tells the reader how the garment gets back into a recycling system.
Making it

Production notes

Edit and length

  • Record the voice-over first, at about 150 words a minute, and cut the animation to it.
  • Full version about 4½ minutes. For a 3-minute cut, drop shots 03 and 15, merge 07 into 06, and shorten 11 to its first two sentences.
  • Music: royalty-free jazz percussion and double bass. Check the licence covers teaching and VLE hosting.
  • Tools: After Effects shape layers with a light "roughen edges" for the cut-paper look, or a browser build if you want it interactive.

Accessibility

  • Captions on, as an SRT file plus burned-in for social. Hold on-screen text at least 3 seconds; six words or fewer per card.
  • No more than three flashes a second (the WCAG flash threshold). The loop "flash" in shot 12 is a single pulse.
  • Colour plus shape for every material, so colour-blind viewers can follow (see key).
  • Put the source list on the Canvas page and on the final card, so students can follow the evidence up.
