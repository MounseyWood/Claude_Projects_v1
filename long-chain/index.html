It begins as oil

Two chemicals made from oil link up, again and again, into one very long chain. Its name is polyethylene terephthalate: PET.

Tap to add a link · 0 links

Lancashire, 1941

John Whinfield and James Dickson invented it at the Calico Printers' Association. Britain called it Terylene. America called it Dacron.

Melt, push, cool, stretch

PET chips are melted and pushed through a spinneret, a plate full of tiny holes. Stretching lines the chains up, and that makes the fibre strong.

The case for

Strong. Holds its shape. Dries fast. Cheap. Takes far less water to make than cotton takes to grow.

Made from oil. Doesn't biodegrade. So cheap it makes overproducing clothes easy. Can feel less breathable.

It sheds

Every wash breaks off tiny fibres. Press Wash, or rub the thread.

0 fibres shed

In one lab study, a 5 kg wash of polyester released more than 6,000,000 microfibres. Some slip through water treatment.

Recycled polyester?

Most recycled polyester starts life as a plastic drinks bottle, not as old clothes.

Add stretch

Elastane, also called spandex, gives clothes their stretch. It isn't polyester. It's polyurethane.

Notes and sources

For tutors, and anyone who wants to check the numbers.

By the end, students can

  1. Say what polyester is: PET, mostly from oil, melt-spun into fibre.
  2. Weigh its pros and cons in apparel.
  3. Explain why blends, especially with elastane, block recycling, and what "mono-material" means.
  4. Describe two routes to stretch without elastane, and ask what evidence backs each claim.

Seminar questions

  1. Your leggings say "78% recycled polyester, 22% elastane". Where can they go at the end of their life? What would you need to find out?
  2. Polyester needs less water than cotton but comes from oil. How would you decide which is better for one particular garment?
  3. PLA composts only in industrial facilities. Is that a real benefit for a UK wardrobe?
  4. A brand says its stretch fibre is "recyclable". What evidence would you ask for, and from whom?
  5. Redesign one stretch garment you own as a mono-material. What do you lose? What do you gain?

How sure are we?

  • Strong Polyester was 59% of fibre in 2024; 88% fossil-based; under 1% of fibre from recycled textiles [1].
  • Strong Invented 1941 by Whinfield and Dickson [2][3].
  • Moderate Less water than cotton: the direction is agreed, the size varies by study [4].
  • Moderate 6,000,000+ microfibres from one 5 kg wash: one lab study [5].
  • Moderate Elastane in ~80% of discarded US clothes: one estimate [6].
  • Moderate 0.1% PLA spoils PET: bottle recycling, 2009 [7].
  • Weak Two-polyester stretch fibre recycles with polyester: the maker's own tests [8][9].
  • Strong PLA melts ~170°C, PET ~250°C [10]. All-polyester rubbers reach ~1,000% elongation (11× length) in the lab [11].
  • Strong EU textile producer responsibility: in force 17 October 2025 [12].

How the thread works

Each frame's thread is a chain of points. Every step, each point keeps moving the way it was already moving, gravity and a breeze nudge it, and then the links are pulled back to their proper length ten times over. That's Verlet integration. To draw a shape, every point is pulled a little towards a target drawing. Text blocks are walls: any point that strays into one is pushed back out.

Think first: what would happen if the links were corrected once per step instead of ten times?
