# Oil to soil? Episode 1 of 3: Made from oil

A bite-size conversation from the Phygital Materials Studio. About 4 minutes. Two AI voices: a curious **Host** and an engaging **Specialist**, reading a script written and checked by the Studio. This script is also the episode's transcript.

**Pairs with:** *The Thread and the Loop*, the Make and Fibre exhibits.
**You'll be able to:** explain what polyester is made from, and how one material becomes very different fibres.

---

**Host:** Before we start: we're both AI voices, reading a script written and checked by the Phygital Materials Studio. So, if you hear us say something, there's a source behind it.

**Specialist:** And the sources are in the notes, so you can check us.

**Host:** Right. Oil to soil. Episode one. I'm holding a T-shirt. The label says polyester. What am I actually holding?

**Specialist:** Mostly, oil and gas. Polyester starts as two chemicals. One is terephthalic acid, which comes from oil. The other is ethylene glycol, which can come from oil or natural gas.

**Host:** Terephthalic. That's a mouthful.

**Specialist:** It is, so people call them TPA and EG. Join them and they link up, again and again, into one very long chain. That chain is PET.

**Host:** PET, like a plastic bottle?

**Specialist:** Exactly like a plastic bottle. Same material. A bottle and a shirt are the same polymer in a different shape. And here's a detail I love: every time two pieces link, a little water is given off.

**Host:** So making polyester makes water?

**Specialist:** A tiny bit, at each link. Hold on to that, because in episode three, water comes back to undo it.

**Host:** When did this start?

**Specialist:** 1941, in Lancashire. Two chemists, John Whinfield and James Dickson, made the first polyester fibre. It was sold as Terylene.

**Host:** And now?

**Specialist:** Now, polyester is 59 per cent of all the fibre the world makes. More than every other fibre put together. And 88 per cent of that polyester is made from fossil fuels.

**Host:** So more than half of everything we wear started as oil or gas.

**Specialist:** More than half of all fibre, yes. That's the scale we're talking about.

**Host:** OK. I've got my chain. How does a chain become a thread?

**Specialist:** You melt it, and push it through a plate full of tiny holes, a bit like a shower head. Each hole makes one filament. Then you stretch it. Stretching lines the chains up, so the fibre gets stronger.

**Host:** And this is where design comes in?

**Specialist:** Already, yes. Change the shape of the hole and you change the fibre. A three-lobed hole makes a fibre that catches the light, so the fabric sparkles. A hollow fibre traps air, so it's warm: that's jacket filling. Grooves along the fibre pull sweat along and help it dry.

**Host:** Same plastic, different jobs.

**Specialist:** Same plastic. And you can choose how many filaments. One thick one is stiff, like fishing line. Lots of very fine ones, each finer than silk, is microfibre. Very soft.

**Host:** So when I touch a soft fleece and a shiny lining, they could both be polyester.

**Specialist:** They often are. That's the power of it, and also the problem. It can be almost anything, so it's everywhere.

**Host:** Why is that a problem?

**Specialist:** Because everything we make has to go somewhere afterwards. And the choices that make polyester so useful decide what can happen to it later. That's episode two.

**Host:** Before we go, one question to take with you. If a bottle and a shirt are the same material, why don't more shirts get made into new shirts?

**Specialist:** Hold that thought. We'll answer it.

**Host:** And if you want to try it with your hands, go to *The Thread and the Loop* in the Phygital Materials Studio and link the chain yourself.
