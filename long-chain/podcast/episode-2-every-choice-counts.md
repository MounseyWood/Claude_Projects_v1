# Oil to soil? Episode 2 of 3: Every choice counts

A bite-size conversation from the Phygital Materials Studio. About 4 minutes. Two AI voices: a curious **Host** and an engaging **Specialist**, reading a script written and checked by the Studio. This script is also the episode's transcript.

**Pairs with:** *The Thread and the Loop*, the Yarn, Combine, For and against, and Wear exhibits.
**You'll be able to:** explain how design choices change a polyester garment, while it's worn and after.

---

**Host:** Quick reminder: we're two AI voices, reading a script written and checked by the Phygital Materials Studio. Episode two. Last time, we made a polyester filament. Now what?

**Specialist:** Now the designer starts making choices, and every one of them counts. First: keep it as a long filament, or chop it short?

**Host:** Why chop it?

**Specialist:** Cut it to about the length of a cotton fibre, then spin it with a twist, and it can feel more like cotton. Softer, less slippery. It can even be spun together with real cotton.

**Host:** A blend.

**Specialist:** A blend. Or you can wrap one fibre round another. Sewing thread often has a polyester core for strength, with cotton outside. And stretch yarns usually hide elastane inside, as the core.

**Host:** Elastane, the stretchy one. Is that polyester too?

**Specialist:** No, and that matters. Elastane is a polyurethane, a different material. Keep that in mind for episode three.

**Host:** Noted. What about colour?

**Specialist:** Polyester fights back. It repels water, so ordinary dyes just sit on top. It needs special dyes, called disperse dyes, and they only get in at about 130 degrees, under pressure.

**Host:** Hotter than boiling.

**Specialist:** Hotter than boiling. Or you put the colour in before the fibre is even made, into the melt. Makers say that saves water, but those figures mostly come from the makers themselves.

**Host:** So ask for the data.

**Specialist:** Always.

**Host:** OK. The shirt's made. I wear it, I wash it. What happens?

**Specialist:** Every wash breaks off tiny fibres. Studies count anywhere from hundreds of thousands to millions of fibres from a single load, depending on the fabric and how they measured. Fine fibres, short staple and loosely twisted yarns tend to shed more.

**Host:** Where do they go?

**Specialist:** Out with the rinse water. In the UK, treatment works catch most of them, around 99 per cent. But what they catch ends up in sewage sludge, and much of that is spread on farmland.

**Host:** So they don't disappear. They move.

**Specialist:** They move. That's the honest version.

**Host:** Then is polyester good or bad?

**Specialist:** I'd push back on the question. Try this. Imagine a raincoat. Polyester is strong, it holds its shape and it dries fast. The evidence for all three is strong, and for a raincoat they really matter.

**Host:** And against?

**Specialist:** Most of it is made from fossil fuels. It barely biodegrades. It sheds fibres. Again, strong evidence. But a raincoat is washed rarely and kept for years, so shedding matters less there.

**Host:** So for a raincoat, polyester might win.

**Specialist:** Might. Now swap it for a cheap T-shirt.

**Host:** Washed every week. Bought often. Doesn't need to be waterproof.

**Specialist:** Exactly. Same reasons, different garment, and the verdict moves the other way. Even "cheap means we buy more" comes into it, though the evidence there is weaker: many experts link cheap fibre to fast fashion, but cause and effect is hard to prove.

**Host:** So the question isn't "is polyester bad?"

**Specialist:** It's "is polyester right for this, and what happens to it next?"

**Host:** Your question to take with you: think of one thing you're wearing right now. Which of today's choices, fibre, blend, colour, would make it hardest to recycle?

**Specialist:** Keep your answer. Episode three tests it.

**Host:** And to play the tug of war yourself, raincoat against T-shirt, go to *The Thread and the Loop* in the Phygital Materials Studio.

## Sources (Studio notes)
- Staple cut near cotton length, about 32–38 mm. Moderate.
- Core-spun sewing thread (polyester core, cotton wrap); elastane usually forms the core of stretch yarns. Moderate and Strong.
- Elastane is polyurethane, not polyester. Strong.
- Disperse dyes at about 130°C under pressure. Strong.
- Solution-dyeing water savings come from producers. Weak, said as such.
- Shedding: hundreds of thousands to millions of fibres per wash, varying by fabric and method (Napper and Thompson 2016; one 2019 lab study); fine, staple and low-twist yarns shed more. Moderate.
- UK treatment works remove about 99% of microplastic particles; removed fibres end up in sludge, much of it spread on farmland (UKWIR 2019). Moderate.
- Strength, shape, fast drying (moisture regain about 0.4% against cotton's 8.5%); fossil-based, barely biodegrades, sheds: Strong. "Cheap means more": Weak, said as such.
- Which reasons matter for which garment is a judgement, offered for discussion.
