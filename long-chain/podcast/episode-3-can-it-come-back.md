# Oil to soil? Episode 3 of 3: Can it come back?

A bite-size conversation from the Phygital Materials Studio. About 4 minutes. Two AI voices: a curious **Host** and an engaging **Specialist**, reading a script written and checked by the Studio. This script is also the episode's transcript.

**Pairs with:** *The Thread and the Loop*, the Return exhibits, from Recycled polyester to Bury it.
**You'll be able to:** explain why most polyester clothing doesn't come back, and what "biodegradable" should have to prove.

---

**Host:** One last reminder: two AI voices, a script written and checked by the Phygital Materials Studio. Episode three. In episode one, I asked: if a bottle and a shirt are the same material, why don't more shirts become new shirts?

**Specialist:** Let's start with what recycled polyester usually is. Most of it was a plastic bottle. Bottles are clean, one material, easy to sort. Clothes are not.

**Host:** And how much clothing becomes new clothing?

**Specialist:** Worldwide, less than one per cent of the material used to make clothing is recycled into new clothing.

**Host:** Less than one per cent. Why so little?

**Specialist:** Remember your answer from episode two? Blends. The simplest recycling is melting: you melt the polyester and spin it again. But if cotton is wound round it, the cotton doesn't melt. If elastane is hidden inside, that's a different material too. The machine jams.

**Host:** Can't you just pull them apart?

**Specialist:** Not easily, not at scale. Separating polycotton is still mostly happening in labs and pilot plants. And even pure polyester gets weaker every time it's melted: the chains get shorter.

**Host:** So melting is a dead end?

**Specialist:** Not a dead end, a limit. There's another route: chemical recycling. Remember the water given off when the chain was made?

**Host:** From episode one, yes.

**Specialist:** Add it back, with heat, or use glycol, or enzymes, and you can break the chain back into its building blocks, then link them up again, as good as new. In 2020, a lab study used an engineered enzyme to break down 90 per cent of PET in about ten hours.

**Host:** That sounds like the answer.

**Specialist:** It's promising. But very few plants run at full scale yet, and whether it's better for the planet depends on the plant and the energy it uses. Studies disagree.

**Host:** OK. What if it never gets recycled? What if it's just buried?

**Specialist:** In one compost test, cotton fabric broke down within months. The polyester stayed intact.

**Host:** And in sunlight?

**Specialist:** Sunlight makes it brittle, so it cracks into smaller and smaller pieces: microplastics. Polyester breaks up, not down. On the sea floor, out of the light, PET bottles barely changed in about 15 years.

**Host:** So, oil to soil?

**Specialist:** For cotton, roughly, yes. For polyester, not yet. Polyester was invented in 1941. No one has yet watched a polyester thread fully disappear. That's reasoning, not a measurement, but it's worth sitting with.

**Host:** What about clothes labelled "biodegradable"?

**Specialist:** Ask three questions. Where? How long? And what's left at the end? UK advertising guidance expects a claim like that to answer all three.

**Host:** Where, how long, what's left. I like that.

**Specialist:** And one more twist. There's a plant-based polyester called PLA that looks exactly like PET. Recyclers treat it as contamination in PET recycling. And it only composts in industrial composters, at around 58 degrees. Not in your garden.

**Host:** So "plant-based" doesn't automatically mean "comes back".

**Specialist:** Exactly. Which brings us to the real question.

**Host:** Go on.

**Specialist:** Imagine every garment you ever designed had to come back to you. Not to a bin. To you.

**Host:** What would you design?

**Specialist:** That's yours to answer.

**Host:** Take that question to *The Thread and the Loop* in the Phygital Materials Studio. Make your own thread, follow it all the way, and see whether it can come back. Thanks for listening.
