The Thread and the Loop

A romance in lower chemistry

A short vertical film for first-year fashion students about polyester: what it is, what it does well and badly, why stretchy clothes rarely come back, and what researchers are trying instead. One blue thread runs down the screen from first frame to last and carries the story.

Before the storyboard

Brief

One thing to remember

A garment can only come back if we know what it's made of, and how it gets back.

Audience

FN4005 undergraduates, Level 4, watching on their phones. No chemistry assumed. Most own polyester and elastane without knowing it, so the care label is the way in.

By the end, students can

  1. Say what polyester is: PET, mostly from oil, melt-spun into fibre.
  2. Weigh its pros and cons in apparel.
  3. Explain why blends, especially with elastane, block recycling, and what "mono-material" means.
  4. Describe two routes to stretch without elastane, and ask what evidence backs each claim.

Tone of voice

Plain, warm and direct. Short sentences. A little wit from the narrator, never at the student's expense. Every claim says who made it, and the film stops where the evidence stops.

No ku-brand skill was found in this account. The palette and type rules come from the KU print rules in the CLO3D worksheet skill. The tone above is inferred, so swap in the official KU tone-of-voice guide if you have it.

Reference

What we take from The Dot and the Line

The film

Chuck Jones and Maurice Noble's 1965 MGM short, from Norton Juster's book and narrated by Robert Morley. A straight line loves a dot, who prefers a wild squiggle. The line teaches itself to bend into angles, shapes and solids, and wins her. It won the 1965 Oscar for animated short [21] [22].

What we borrow

  • Shapes are the characters. A thread, a loop, a squiggle, a lookalike. No people.
  • One narrator tells it like a story. All meaning comes through voice-over and movement.
  • The line learns to bend. Our thread draws 1941, a shirt, a bottle, a grid and finally a spring. Bending is how it solves its problem.
  • A rough, boiling squiggle. Jones inked the squiggle on rice paper so it bled [21]. Our elastane is a jittery, hairy black line.
  • Colour blocking for mood. White grounds explain; black grounds carry problems and turning points.

What we don't

  • No characters, music, lines or narrator imitation from the original.
  • No happy-ever-after. The film's line wins. Our thread hasn't yet, because the evidence says it hasn't.
Shapes as characters

Cast

The Thread

Polyester (PET). KU blue, always on screen, always flowing downward. Strong and dependable, a bit stiff.

The Loop

Coming back: recycling into new fibre. A black ring. The thread wants to join it.

Elastane

The squiggle. Polyurethane, not polyester. Exciting, stretchy, in almost everything, and it jams the loop.

PLA

The lookalike. A polyester made from plants. A dashed blue line: almost the same as the thread, which is the problem.

Corrections and context

Corrections to the first research draft

  • Same polyester family does not mean same recycling stream. PLA contaminates PET recycling [8]. Now shot 12.
  • The Sorona® evidence was a supplier's sales page. Replaced by the two-polyester mechanism, with the recycling claim labelled as the maker's.
  • EcoLASTANE is historical: an EU FP7 project, from a programme that closed to new projects in 2013. Check the CORDIS record before citing it.
  • The PLA stretch-yarn patent dates from 2011. A patent is a claim, not a product.
  • Left out as unverified: market-report claims about new PLA stretch fibres and PLA price premiums.

Context (not in the film)

  • Textile-to-textile polyester recycling is scaling slowly. Syre plans to start building a plant of up to 250,000 t/yr in Vietnam in 2027 [16]. Carbios has missed its financing target for its Longlaville plant [17].
  • PEF, another plant-based polyester. Avantium's FDCA plant has made its first batches; commercial sales are expected from late 2026 [18].
  • Mono-material helps most within one group: cellulose, polyamide or polyester [19].
After the screening

Seminar questions

Discuss

  1. Your leggings say "78% recycled polyester, 22% elastane". Where can they go at the end of their life? What would you need to find out?
  2. Polyester needs less water than cotton but comes from oil. How would you decide which is better for one particular garment?
  3. PLA is compostable, but only in industrial facilities. Is that a real benefit for a UK wardrobe?
  4. A brand says its stretch fibre is "recyclable". What evidence would you ask for, and from whom?
  5. A report says recycled polyester sheds more microplastics than virgin [6]. Who paid for it, how was it tested, and how much weight should it carry?

Make

  1. Pick one stretch garment you own. Redesign it as a mono-material. List what you lose and what you gain.
  2. Draw a care label that also tells the reader how the garment gets back into a recycling system.
Making it

How the thread works, and production notes

Verlet thread, in plain words

  • The thread is 110 points joined in a chain. Each frame, every point keeps moving the way it was already moving, plus a little gravity and a gentle sideways breeze.
  • Then the links are pulled back to their proper length, twelve times over. That's what makes it behave like thread and not like loose beads.
  • To draw a shape, each point is nudged a little towards a target drawing (the digits, the shirt, the spring). The links drag their neighbours along, so the thread swings into place.
  • Flow ticks slide along it top to bottom so it always reads as running down the screen.

Think first: what would happen to the thread if the links were only corrected once per frame instead of twelve times?

Production

  • Record the voice-over first, at about 150 words a minute, then fit the animation to it. The browser voice here is only a pacing guide.
  • To export: open long-chain/index.html from the repo directly in Chrome on your computer. A "Record 1080×1920" button appears there (it can't work inside a hosted page) and saves a WebM to edit in Premiere or CapCut.
  • Captions: use "Copy SRT captions" above, and burn them in above the bottom safe zone for social versions.
  • No more than three flashes a second. The hoop flash in shot 12 is one pulse.
  • Music: royalty-free, light and rhythmic. Check the licence covers teaching and VLE hosting.
  • Short cut (about 3 minutes): drop shots 03 and 15, merge 07 into 06.
