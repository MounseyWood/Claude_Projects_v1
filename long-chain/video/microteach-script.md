# The Thread and the Loop: 3-minute microteach

Narration script for the motion-graphic version of the lab. 16:9, about 3:00, AI-generated voice. Every spoken claim is rated Strong in the lab's notes except the sunlight line [10], which is Moderate (lab studies, not field measurements); the source for each line is given in brackets and listed at the end.

**Learning outcome:** explain why most polyester does not come back, and how design choices decide that.

**Voice direction (paste into Google AI Studio, Gemini text-to-speech):** "Read in a calm, clear, warm British voice. Unhurried, about 140 words a minute. Short pause at each full stop, longer pause at each line break."

---

## 0:00–0:15 · Hook and outcome
*Screen: the thread unwinds from its spool on the title page.*

This is one polyester thread. In the next three minutes, we'll follow it from oil to the ground.

By the end, you'll be able to explain why most polyester never comes back.

## 0:15–0:50 · Make
*Screen: Link the chain. The hand taps; TPA and EG link; the thread writes PET. Large type: 1941 · 59% · 88%.*

Polyester starts as oil and gas. [2]

Two chemicals, terephthalic acid and ethylene glycol, link up again and again into one long chain: PET. Each link gives off a little water. [2][3]

It was invented in Lancashire in 1941. [4]

Today, polyester is 59 per cent of all the fibre the world makes. And 88 per cent of that polyester comes from fossil fuels. [1]

## 0:50–1:25 · Shape
*Screen: melt and stretch; nozzle; cut and twist; wrap; dye bath rising to 130°C.*

The chain is melted, pushed through tiny holes, and stretched into filament. [5]

Here, designers make choices. Cut it short and spin it, and it can feel like cotton. Twist it. Ply it. Wrap it in another fibre.

Dye it, and it fights back: polyester repels water, so its dyes only get in at about 130 degrees, under pressure. [6]

Every choice changes how it feels. And every choice decides what can happen to it later.

## 1:25–1:40 · Pause and predict
*Screen: the thread, wrapped in cotton, at the recycler. On-screen: "Pause. Predict." Hold 5 seconds.*

Pause here. This thread is wrapped in cotton. Will the recycler take it?

Decide. Then play.

## 1:40–2:20 · Return
*Screen: Feed the recycler. The ring jams. Then the bottle becomes a T-shirt; the loop breaks into a tangle.*

It jams. Blends are hard to separate, so most blended clothing can't go round again. [7]

Most recycled polyester doesn't come from clothes at all. It comes from plastic bottles. [1]

Worldwide, less than one per cent of the material used to make clothing is recycled into new clothing. [8]

## 2:20–2:40 · Bury
*Screen: Bury it. The heap warms, cotton is eaten, polyester stays; the scrap in the sun cracks.*

So what if we bury it? In a compost test, cotton fabric broke down within months. The polyester stayed intact. [9]

In sunlight, polyester becomes brittle and cracks into smaller and smaller pieces. It breaks up, not down. [10]

## 2:40–3:00 · Check and reflect
*Screen: three questions, each held 3 seconds, then the answer. Then the end question in the big loop, and the lab link.*

Three quick questions. What is PET made from? Why does a blend jam the recycler? Where does most recycled polyester come from?

Now, one question for you. What would you design, if every garment had to come back to you?

Try the lab, and find out.

## Check yourself (Canvas quiz, 5 questions)

1. PET is made by linking which two chemicals? **Terephthalic acid and ethylene glycol.** (Distractors: cellulose and water; nylon and elastane; oil and salt.)
2. Roughly what share of the world's fibre is polyester? **About 59%.** (Distractors: about 10%; about 30%; about 90%.)
3. Why does a cotton-wrapped polyester thread jam a mechanical recycler? **Two fibres are bound together and can't be melted as one.** (Distractors: cotton melts first; the dye blocks it; it is too thin.)
4. Where does most recycled polyester come from? **Plastic bottles.** (Distractors: old clothes; factory offcuts; fishing nets.)
5. What happens to polyester in sunlight? **It cracks into smaller pieces (microplastics), not into soil.** (Distractors: it rots like cotton; it dissolves; nothing at all.)
