s1|This is one polyester thread. In the next three minutes, we'll follow it from oil to soil. Or will we? By the end, you'll be able to explain why most polyester never comes back.
s2|Polyester starts as oil and gas. Two chemicals, terephthalic acid and ethylene glycol, link up again and again into one long chain: PET. Each link gives off a little water. It was invented in Lancashire in 1941. Today, polyester is 59 per cent of all the fibre the world makes. And 88 per cent of that polyester comes from fossil fuels.
s3|The chain is melted, pushed through tiny holes, and stretched into filament. Here, designers make choices. Cut it short and spin it, and it can feel like cotton. Twist it. Ply it. Wrap it in another fibre. Dye it, and it fights back: polyester repels water, so its dyes only get in at about 130 degrees, under pressure. Every choice changes how it feels. And every choice decides what can happen to it later.
s5|It jams. Blends are hard to separate, so most blended clothing can't go round again. Most recycled polyester doesn't come from clothes at all. It comes from plastic bottles. Worldwide, less than one per cent of the material used to make clothing is recycled into new clothing.
s6|So what if we bury it? In a compost test, cotton fabric broke down within months. The polyester stayed intact. In sunlight, polyester becomes brittle and cracks into smaller and smaller pieces. It breaks up, not down. So: oil to soil? Not polyester. Not yet.
s7|Three quick questions. What is PET made from? Why does a blend jam the recycler? Where does most recycled polyester come from? Now, one question for you. What would you design, if every garment had to come back to you? Try the lab, and find out.
