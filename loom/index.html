The notation

Four blocks and one grid

The layout on the left is not a diagram of a loom. It is how weavers have written cloth down for centuries, and every part of it is doing a job.

Top left · threading

Which shaft each warp thread passes through

One filled square per column. The loom lifts shafts, not individual threads, so this is what decides which threads rise together. Click a column to move that thread to another shaft.

Top right · tie-up

Which shafts each treadle lifts

Eight treadles, eight shafts, sixty-four squares. This is the part that is literally a stored program, and the part Jacquard would later punch onto a card. Click to toggle.

Bottom right · treadling

The order you press them

One square per pick, read downwards as the cloth grows. Change it and the same threading and tie-up will give you a different cloth — a twill becomes a herringbone this way.

Bottom left · drawdown

What you will get

Not editable, because it is not an input. A square is filled when the treadle for that pick lifts the shaft that thread is on. That single boolean is the entire structure of the cloth.

The bias

Pull it diagonally and it is a different fabric

Set the tension to something you can see, then drag the angle from 0° to 90° — or press sweep the angle and watch the plot fill in. The curve peaks at 45°, and it is not a small peak.

Watch the yarn-stretched gauge while you do it. It stays near zero. Nothing is being stretched. On the bias the threads rotate at the crossings and the square grid leans over into a diamond, which is longer corner to corner than it was — a trellis gate, not a spring. That is why a bias-cut skirt falls the way it does, and why the same cloth cut on the grain will not do it.
On the grain

You are pulling on the threads

What give there is comes from crimp: the warp lies in a wave over and under the weft, and a pull straightens that wave before it ever loads the fibre. Watch warp crimp fall and weft crimp rise as you pull — the two systems trade it. Once the wave is out, the cloth is as long as it is going to get.

On the bias

You are closing a hinge

Every crossing is a pivot. The cloth gives until the threads swing far enough to touch their neighbours and jam, and then it stops dead. That angle is not a setting on this page — it falls out of the thread thickness and the spacing. Thicken the thread and the give disappears.

One weave in particular

Why your jeans are blue outside and pale inside

Load 3/1 twill, look at the face, then press show the back.

The face is nearly all indigo. The back is nearly all undyed. Nothing about the threads changed between those two pictures — the warp is indigo in both. What changed is which system is on top. In a 3/1 twill each warp thread floats over three picks and dips under one, so it covers three quarters of one face and a quarter of the other.

That is denim, and it is the whole recipe: indigo warp, undyed weft, 3/1 twill. The pale inside of a pair of jeans is not a lining. It is the same cloth seen from the other side.

And it is why denim fades where it does. Indigo is a poor penetrator — it rings the outside of the yarn and leaves the core white. The warp floats are the part standing proud of the surface, so they take the abrasion, lose their indigo first, and expose the pale core. The wear follows the twill line because the floats do.
Small print

What is derived, and what is dialled

Worth separating, because a simulation that quietly dials in its own conclusions is not showing you anything.

Derived from your draft

Nobody chose these

Which thread is on top at each of the crossings — that is the drawdown itself. Crimp, and with it how much the cloth can give on the grain. Float length, and so how much of each face is warp and how much is weft. The locking angle, from thread thickness against spacing.

Dialled in

Chosen, and worth doubting

Grip at the crossings is the one number with no derivation here — it stands in for inter-thread friction, and it sets how freely the bias gives. It is on a slider so you can see what it is doing. Yarn stretch and bending stiffness are also chosen, though the cloth is barely sensitive to the first.

The dialled number turns out to matter less than it looks. Loosening the grip makes the bias give more freely — up to a point, and then it stops mattering entirely, because the threads have swung far enough to touch each other and jam. Past that the cloth is held by its own geometry, and geometry is not on a slider.

Plain weave at 2 N/m. A thousandfold change in the one number this model takes on faith moves the answer by a tenth of a percent, once jamming has taken over. The locking angle at this sett is 31.8° of shear, computed from thread thickness against pitch before the simulation starts — and the measured mean tops out just under it. Note also that the grain column barely moves at all: extension along the grain is crimp coming out, and crimp has nothing to do with how freely the crossings turn.

Threads do not pass through each other. There is no general collision detection here and none is needed: the draft already says which threads cross and in what order, so every contact pair is known before the simulation starts. That is what makes this cheap enough to run in a browser.
The crossings do not slip. Warp and weft are held together in the plane at every crossing. Real threads can slide, which is how a loose weave distorts and how seams grin. Modelling that would need the grip to be a limit rather than a spring.
The swatch is drawn coarse. Eighteen threads each way at a three-millimetre pitch is about a sixth of the sett of real shirting. It is drawn that way so you can see the interlacement; a real cloth of this construction would have far finer threads far closer together.
Mass is scaled. A swatch this size weighs almost nothing, so any realistic tension would be a violent acceleration rather than a load. Gravity is off here, so the mass only changes how quickly the cloth reaches equilibrium, not where the equilibrium is.
Postscript

The grid

Everything you changed on the left of this page was a grid of filled and empty squares. Nothing else went into the cloth.

In 1804 Joseph-Marie Jacquard put that grid onto punched cards and hung them off a loom, so a pattern could be stored, copied, and sent somewhere else. The cards outlived the looms. Herman Hollerith used them to tabulate the 1890 United States census, and the punched card stayed the ordinary way of feeding a machine for most of the century that followed.

Ada Lovelace saw it in 1843, writing that the Analytical Engine “weaves algebraical patterns just as the Jacquard-loom weaves flowers and leaves.”
