Three things to try

The fabric selector only works on one of them

Each button drives the controls and reads the gauges back. The number reported is mean tensile strain — how far the average grid edge has been pulled past its drafted length, counting tension only, since cloth in compression just buckles and carries no load.

One pass, then sixteen

Same again with iterations instead of substeps. Both solvers start under‑converged and both improve. Only one of them stops somewhere: XPBD lands on its fabric and holds, Verlet keeps stiffening for as long as you keep paying.

The difference

One multiplier, replaced

Both solvers walk the same list of distance constraints and nudge the two endpoints towards the drafted length. The integrator is near enough identical either way — the difference you are watching lives entirely in how far each nudge goes.

Verlet · Jakobsen relaxation1967 / 2001
// per constraint, per pass
d  = p2 - p1
L  = |d|
C  = L - rest

s  = k * C / L          // k in [0,1]

p1 += s * w1/(w1+w2) * d
p2 -= s * w2/(w1+w2) * d
k is a relaxation factor, not a material. Its effect compounds: after n passes the cloth behaves as though its stiffness were roughly 1 − (1 − k)ⁿ. Change n and you have changed the fabric.
XPBD · compliant constraint2016
// per constraint, per pass
d  = p2 - p1
L  = |d|
C  = L - rest

a_t = alpha / (dt*dt)     // compliance / step²
dl  = (-C - a_t*lam) / (w1 + w2 + a_t)
lam += dl                 // kept across passes

p1 -= w1 * dl * d/L
p2 += w2 * dl * d/L
alpha is inverse stiffness in m/N. At rest the solver settles on C = α × force — Hooke’s law, arrived at by projection. Neither Δt nor the pass count appears in that result.
Why lam is the whole trick. Position-based solvers throw the constraint force away every pass, so the only thing holding the shirt up is however much error the last pass happened to remove. XPBD accumulates that force in lam and subtracts what it has already spent, so extra passes converge towards a fixed answer instead of pushing towards infinite stiffness. Reset lam once per substep, never per pass.
Checking the claim

A hanging chain has a right answer

A shirt is hard to argue about. A chain of twelve links is not: link i carries the weight below it, so its tension is known, and a spring of compliance α must sit at extension α × T. For this chain the closed form gives 15.304 mm of total elongation. Both solvers on this page were run against it — the same solver source the stage above runs, driven headlessly — at the budgets the sliders offer.

XPBD is loose at the smallest budgets — too soft rather than too stiff — and closes on the analytic answer from there, holding it to within about one percent however the remaining budget is spent. Verlet passes through the right answer on its way somewhere else and keeps going: every increment of budget takes it further from the material and closer to a rigid rod. Neither column is a bug. They are the two solvers doing exactly what they are defined to do.

An honest caveat. Below roughly α = 8e−4 the shirt’s elongation drops beneath what the solver can resolve at any budget this page will run, so poplin, melton, denim and canvas all read within a few hundredths of a percent of each other. That floor is the solver’s residual, not the fabric — the stiff end of the range needs a finer mesh and a bigger budget to tell apart.
For the cutting room

Compliance is a number you can specify

This is the part that matters if you ever want a simulation to agree with a toile. Compliance has units — metres of extension per newton of load — so it survives being written on a spec, sent to someone else, and run on a different machine at a different framerate. Load any row into the stage above and watch which shirt notices.

FabricStretch αBend αReads as

These are demo values chosen to span a useful range and to look right on a 3 cm mesh — they are not measurements. Real numbers come off a KES‑F or FAST rig as tensile and bending moduli, and convert into per‑edge compliance once you know your mesh spacing. That conversion is possible because the parameter has units. There is no equivalent conversion for a Verlet relaxation factor.

Construction

How the shirt gets built

Four steps, in order — each one depends on the last. There is no imported mesh here; the block is drafted in code from two outline functions and everything else falls out of it.

STEP 01

Draft

A dropped‑shoulder T‑block as two curves over the pattern coordinate: a top line that is the neck scoop until it becomes the shoulder slope, and a bottom line that is the hem until it becomes the underarm and tapers to the cuff.

STEP 02

Digitise

Sample on a regular grid. Cells whose centre falls between the two curves survive; anything left hanging by a single edge is trimmed away. Surviving cells become quads, their corners become particles.

STEP 03

Seam

Front and back are the same grid. Where a boundary particle sits on a shoulder, side seam or underarm it is welded — one particle, shared by both panels. Neck, cuffs and hem are left as free edges, so the garment is open where a garment is open.

STEP 04

Inflate

A breadth‑first walk inwards from the seams gives every particle its distance from the nearest join. Push front and back apart by that distance and the flat lay becomes a body, pinched to nothing exactly along the stitching.

What the mesh carries. Grid edges are stretch constraints, cell diagonals are shear, and every second particle along a row or column is joined as a bend constraint with rest length taken from the inflated shape — so the block remembers its own volume rather than trying to flatten. Three compliances, three fabric behaviours: how much it grows, how much it skews on the bias, how tightly it folds.
Small print

What this is not doing

No self‑collision. The shirt passes through itself. Real cloth solvers spend most of their budget here; adding it would swamp the thing this page exists to show.
Bending is distance‑based. A proper dihedral‑angle constraint is better behaved under large folds. Two‑apart distance constraints are cheap, stable, and adequate at this scale.
The form is seven ellipsoids. Collision projects along the scaled radial direction rather than to the true closest point — fine for a demo, wrong for a fitting.
Verlet is being run fairly. Same mesh, same constraints, same substeps, same passes, same gravity, and by default the highest stiffness it can take. Every difference on screen comes from the constraint rule alone.
